<figure><img src="../../../media/publications/birds-catchup-20261104-d/bubo-ascalaphus.jpg" width="1180" height="1191" alt="Pharaoh Eagle-Owl — Bubo ascalaphus" loading="lazy" decoding="async" style="display:block;width:100%;height:auto"><figcaption>Pharaoh Eagle-Owl</figcaption></figure><p class="excerpt">Bubo ascalaphus</p><h2>Identity and identification</h2><p>Pharaoh Eagle-Owl Bubo ascalaphus belongs to Strigidae. A large desert owl, it has sandy mottled plumage, orange-yellow eyes and head feather tufts. It differs from larger Eurasian Eagle-Owl, but dark photographs alone cannot reliably separate owls. The tufts are feathers, not external ears.</p><h2>Habitat, food and behaviour</h2><p>Cliffs, wadis and rocky desert provide habitat. Nocturnal hunting takes rodents, birds, reptiles and other locally available prey. Specialised wings permit quiet approaches. A bird resting near a cavity by day is not necessarily ill.</p><h2>Breeding and parental care</h2><p>It nests on cliffs and in sheltered cavities, sometimes using old raven nests, according to Abu Dhabi’s Environment Agency. Young depend on adult prey deliveries and site protection. Quiet habitat and food determine success; cavities must not be illuminated to count eggs or chicks.</p><h2>Range and Arab occurrence</h2><p>It is resident in parts of North Africa and Arabia, with occurrence and monitoring documented in Abu Dhabi. Suitable desert distribution does not mean equal presence in all Arab countries. New records require face, eye and structure comparisons alongside location.</p><h2>Seasonality and observation</h2><p>Residents can be observed in November. Listening at a distance is preferable to approach or repeated call playback. Exact cavity locations should not be publicised if they would attract disturbance to a sensitive breeding site.</p><h2>Ecological role and conservation</h2><p>It consumes desert rodents and other prey and faces poisoning, hunting and cliff destruction. Rodenticides can poison predators through prey. Protecting cavities, reducing toxicants and respecting nocturnal activity help; repeated flash or deliberate luring should be avoided.</p><h2>References</h2><p>Environment Agency–Abu Dhabi — Pharaoh Eagle Owl.</p><p class="photo-credit">Photo: Jean-Paul Boerekamps — CC0 1.0.</p>
