<figure><img src="../../../media/publications/birds-catchup-20261104-c/riparia-riparia.jpg" width="2048" height="1366" alt="Sand Martin — Riparia riparia" loading="lazy" decoding="async" style="display:block;width:100%;height:auto"><figcaption>Sand Martin</figcaption></figure><p class="excerpt">Riparia riparia</p><h2>Identity and identification</h2><p>Sand Martin Riparia riparia belongs to Hirundinidae. Small and brown above, it has white underparts crossed by a distinct brown breast band. This band, head shape and flight separate it from white-rumped House Martin. Closely related sand martins may require more detailed comparison. It is placed in the order Passeriformes. The IUCN category carried in AviList 2026 is Least Concern (LC).</p><h2>Habitat, food and behaviour</h2><p>It catches flying insects above water, fields and wet plains. Migrants gather at feeding and roosting sites, sometimes using reeds for sleep. Concentrations reflect food and resting opportunities rather than proving a nesting colony.</p><h2>Breeding and parental care</h2><p>A horizontal burrow is excavated into a sandy or earthen face, often in colonies. BTO gives four or five eggs, shared incubation around 14–15 days and young leaving at about 19–21 days. Two broods are possible. A stable bank and unobstructed entrance are essential.</p><h2>Range and Arab occurrence</h2><p>It breeds widely in temperate regions and winters farther south. Lebanon has common passage; possible nesting near the Assi remained unconfirmed in the 2020 checklist. Large migrant numbers must not be equated with residence or breeding.</p><h2>Seasonality and observation</h2><p>Lebanese passage extends through February–May and August–November. November falls within the end of autumn passage. Multiple views confirm the breast band; counts must avoid repeatedly counting circling birds.</p><h2>Ecological role and conservation</h2><p>Bank destruction while occupied, polluted water and insect declines are pressures. Stable nesting faces away from hazardous work help. Observe burrows from a distance: standing at entrances prevents parents from provisioning young.</p><h2>References</h2><p>Ramadan-Jaradi et al. — Updated checklist of the birds of Lebanon, Sandgrouse 42 (2020), 186–238.</p><p>British Trust for Ornithology — BirdFacts: Sand Martin.</p><p>AviList Core Team — AviList: The Global Avian Checklist, v2026 (20 October 2026).</p><p class="photo-credit">Photo: Braden J.
