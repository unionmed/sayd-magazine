<figure><img src="../../../media/publications/daily-birds-20261110/mergus-serrator.jpg" width="1567" height="1175" alt="Female-type Red-breasted Merganser swimming on water" loading="lazy" decoding="async" style="display:block;width:100%;height:auto"><figcaption>Red-breasted Merganser</figcaption></figure><p class="excerpt">Mergus serrator</p><h2>Identity, life and distribution</h2><p>Red-breasted Merganser Mergus serrator has a slender, serrated bill suited to holding fish. Both sexes carry a shaggy crest: the male’s head is dark and glossy, the female’s reddish brown. Bill shape and neck-pattern boundaries matter when separating similar sawbills; the crest alone is insufficient.</p><p>It dives for food and uses both coastal and inland waters, with marine habitats important outside the breeding season. Breeding is mainly northern. BTO gives a typical clutch of 8–10 eggs. Young birds can move and swim before they acquire full flight capability.</p><p>The nest is on the ground in a sheltered place near water. The female incubates for about 31–32 days, while young typically fledge at 60–65 days in BTO’s reference data. Their extended dependence on aquatic habitats makes undisturbed shorelines important during brood-rearing.</p><p>Its occurrence varies among Arab countries. SPNL published a record in 2019 described as Lebanon’s second. The Red List assessment lists Kuwait, Oman and Saudi Arabia among countries of vagrant occurrence, contrasting with its regular presence in parts of its northern range.</p><p>Its global status is Least Concern, LC; the European assessment differs. Water degradation, pollution and disturbance at resting sites remain relevant concerns, and a few sightings cannot describe the condition of an entire species.</p><p class="photo-credit">Photo: Hamilton Turner</p>
