<figure><img src="../../../media/publications/birds-catchup-20261104-a/carduelis-carduelis.jpg" width="1080" height="810" alt="European Goldfinch — Carduelis carduelis" loading="lazy" decoding="async" style="display:block;width:100%;height:auto"><figcaption>European Goldfinch</figcaption></figure><p class="excerpt">Carduelis carduelis</p><h2>Identity and identification</h2><p>European Goldfinch Carduelis carduelis belongs to Fringillidae. Adults combine a red mask, black-and-white head and broad yellow wing bar; juveniles lack the red mask. The pointed conical bill extracts small seeds. A yellow wing bar alone does not distinguish it from European Greenfinch.</p><h2>Habitat, food and behaviour</h2><p>Orchards, farmland, woodland edges and gardens provide habitat, especially where seed-bearing plants remain. It clings to thistle heads to extract seeds and also takes insects, particularly when feeding young. Outside breeding, mobile flocks announce themselves with bright, liquid calls.</p><h2>Breeding and parental care</h2><p>The female builds a neat cup among tree branches. BTO gives a typical clutch of five eggs, incubated by the female for 12–14 days. Young leave at about 14–17 days after hatching and remain dependent on feeding afterwards. Two broods are possible; local climate and geography determine laying dates.</p><h2>Range and Arab occurrence</h2><p>Its natural range spans Europe, western Asia and North Africa. It is familiar in the Maghreb and Levant. Lebanon’s updated checklist describes a common breeding resident supplemented by passage and winter immigration. That status does not imply native residence throughout the Gulf.</p><h2>Seasonality and observation</h2><p>It can be watched throughout the year in resident areas, with flocks particularly apparent in autumn and winter. Lebanese breeding principally extends from spring into summer. November observations fit resident presence and winter additions, rather than demonstrating late nesting.</p><h2>Ecological role and conservation</h2><p>It consumes seeds and insects and benefits from varied wild vegetation. Illegal capture and trade, together with loss of edge habitats, are pressures. Leaving thistles and other seed plants to mature and reducing pesticides maintain food supplies; wild observation supports a different relationship from cage keeping.</p><h2>References</h2><p>Ramadan-Jaradi et al. — Updated checklist of the birds of Lebanon, Sandgrouse 42 (2020), 186–238.</p><p>British Trust for Ornithology — BirdFacts: Goldfinch.</p><p class="photo-credit">Photo: Møl Herold — CC0 1.0.</p>
