<figure><img src="../../../media/publications/birds-catchup-20261104-b/coracias-garrulus.jpg" width="2048" height="1373" alt="European Roller — Coracias garrulus" loading="lazy" decoding="async" style="display:block;width:100%;height:auto"><figcaption>European Roller</figcaption></figure><p class="excerpt">Coracias garrulus</p><h2>Identity and identification</h2><p>European Roller Coracias garrulus belongs to Coraciidae. Adults have a blue head and body, a reddish-brown back and darker blue flight feathers. Juveniles are duller. Its heavier build and stronger bill separate it from bee-eaters, and it lacks their elongated pointed central tail feathers. It is placed in the order Coraciiformes. The IUCN category carried in AviList 2026 is Least Concern (LC).</p><h2>Habitat, food and behaviour</h2><p>Open farmland and grassland with scattered trees, wires or branches provide lookout posts. It drops to the ground for large insects and sometimes takes lizards and other small prey. Tumbling courtship displays explain its English name but differ from routine flight between perches.</p><h2>Breeding and parental care</h2><p>It nests in tree cavities or suitable walls and banks, sometimes accepting nest boxes in breeding areas. Both parents contribute to incubation and insect provisioning. Dependence on cavities makes old trees important: available hunting ground alone cannot support a nesting pair.</p><h2>Range and Arab occurrence</h2><p>It breeds across parts of Europe, western Asia and North Africa and winters in sub-Saharan Africa. Lebanon’s 2020 checklist calls it a common passage migrant without confirmed recent breeding. A bird passing in spring does not demonstrate local nesting.</p><h2>Seasonality and observation</h2><p>Lebanese passage usually extends from late March to early June and early August to late October. November follows the usual autumn peak. Dates vary across the Arab region; dated records are more useful than imposing a single regional season.</p><h2>Ecological role and conservation</h2><p>Large-insect predation links it to varied, low-pesticide farmland. Loss of hollow trees, agricultural intensification, pesticides and illegal shooting are pressures. Preserving cavities and insect-rich feeding areas along migration routes supports the species while leaving nests and resting birds undisturbed.</p><h2>References</h2><p>Ramadan-Jaradi et al. — Updated checklist of the birds of Lebanon, Sandgrouse 42 (2020), 186–238.</p><p>Royal Commission for AlUla — Birds of AlUla, European Roller.</p><p>AviList Core Team — AviList: The Global Avian Checklist, v2026 (20 October 2026).</p><p class="photo-credit">Photo: Лариса Артемьева — CC0 1.0.</p>
